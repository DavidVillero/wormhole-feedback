Traveller

One shell of ordinary matter comes in from the right, 20 throat radii away, and reaches the throat at t = 20. The controller sits at distance 1. With the detector on, the controller measures the traveller as it passes the mouth and cancels it as it goes; with it off, the controller only reacts afterwards. In our tests the detector run held up to mass 0.01 and lost 0.0125.

Probe

A small object launched from 6 throat radii away on the right. It follows the exact path of a freely falling body in the spacetime being computed, but it is too light to bend that spacetime, so the controller does not feel it. Aim inside the mouth (less than 1 throat radius off centre) and it passes through; aim wider and it swings round and comes back. “With a traveller” also sends the shell of matter from the second tab, which the controller does have to fight; switching restarts the run. What is being computed

  • The full Einstein equations for a spherically symmetric spacetime holding two fields: the exotic (phantom) field that keeps the Ellis-Bronnikov wormhole open, and ordinary matter. Your browser solves them in real time on a grid of 3,600 points out to 90 throat radii, and checks the Einstein constraints as it goes.
  • This is the same code we validated in Python: it reproduces those runs to a few parts in a billion, pulse for pulse.
  • A traveller run solves both sides of the wormhole (4,801 points out to 60 throat radii each way), because one traveller breaks the mirror symmetry. The controller then reads the throat where it is narrowest, which drifts in these coordinates after a crossing, and fires its pulses around that point. Checked against the Python runs at masses 0.005, 0.01 and 0.0125: same outcomes, the same time of loss (55.4), and peak throat offsets of 1.9% vs 1.9% and 6.7% vs 8.8%. The outer edges differ, so the match is not exact.
  • A probe is a test particle: its path is the geodesic equation solved on the same grid, step for step with the spacetime. On the wormhole at rest it reproduces the known answers (straight-line radial fall, turning back at the right radius when aimed wide, energy conserved to 2 parts in 100,000). It has no gravity of its own, so the wormhole and the controller ignore it.
  • Every pulse, from the controller or from you, is added as a shell of field with the geometry re-solved around it, so the spacetime always satisfies Einstein's equations.
  • The controller is the Kalman filter and regulator from our tests. It sees only the throat's radius and how fast it is changing, delayed by the light-travel time, and it ignores the first 10 time units, which carry a start-up transient.
  • Lengths are in throat radii; times are light-crossing times of the throat. The drawing is the shape space would have if it could be embedded in flat space, with small changes magnified so you can see them.

How far away can the controller sit?

The further away it is, the longer the delay, and the more exactly it must know how the throat responds. Each extra throat radius of distance tightens the requirement about tenfold.

Here the grid itself is a source of model error: the controller was calibrated on this same grid, but the slow numerical drift of the wormhole still counts against its margin. In our tests that is enough to defeat it at distance 2 and beyond.

Limits: spherical symmetry only (the throat can only breathe), perfect sensors and pulses, and phantom matter with negative energy, which nothing says exists. When a singularity forms the solver has to stop.
